5G NEW RADIO

5G NR — Complete Engineering Guide

A deep practical guide to 5G NR architecture, radio procedures, protocol layers, 5GC, QoS, mobility, security and modern 5G evolution.

1. 5G NR at a glance

5G New Radio (NR) is the radio access technology of 5G. It is designed for flexible spectrum use, scalable numerology, beam-centric operation, massive MIMO, low latency, high capacity and a service-based 5G Core.

The engineering model is best understood as two connected domains: NG-RAN (UE + gNB) and the 5G Core (5GC). NR handles radio connectivity; the core handles registration, mobility, authentication, sessions, policy, charging and user-plane routing.

2. System architecture

UE connects to the gNB over Uu. The gNB connects to the 5GC through NG-C (control plane) and NG-U (user plane). In a typical split gNB, the RU handles RF/low-PHY work, the DU handles lower-layer processing, MAC and RLC, and the CU handles higher layers such as PDCP/SDAP/RRC.

  • 5GC functions: AMF, SMF, UPF, AUSF, UDM, PCF, NRF, NSSF and related functions.
  • Control plane: NAS + RRC + NGAP.
  • User plane: SDAP + PDCP + RLC + MAC + PHY.

3. SA, NSA and dual connectivity

Standalone (SA) uses NR with the 5GC. Non-Standalone (NSA) combines LTE/EPC or LTE/NR dual-connectivity arrangements while operators transition to SA. Important concepts include master/secondary nodes, master/secondary cell groups, split bearers, EN-DC, NE-DC and other MR-DC configurations.

Do not confuse the radio generation with the core generation: a network may provide an NR radio while still relying on LTE/EPC-era architecture in an NSA deployment.

4. Important interfaces

  • Uu: UE ↔ gNB radio interface.
  • NG-C / N2: gNB ↔ AMF control plane.
  • NG-U / N3: gNB ↔ UPF user plane.
  • Xn: gNB ↔ gNB/NG-RAN node coordination, including mobility.
  • N1: UE ↔ AMF NAS signalling.
  • N2: RAN ↔ AMF.
  • N3: RAN ↔ UPF.
  • N4: SMF ↔ UPF.
  • N6: UPF ↔ Data Network.
  • Nxx/SBI: service-based interactions among 5GC network functions.

5. Spectrum and deployment

NR spans low-band coverage, mid-band capacity and mmWave high-capacity deployments. 3GPP commonly groups operation into FR1 and FR2. Lower frequencies provide better coverage and penetration; higher frequencies offer more bandwidth but require tighter beam management and generally face higher propagation loss.

Engineering decisions include channel bandwidth, TDD pattern, carrier aggregation, supplementary downlink, beamforming, power limits and coexistence constraints.

6. Numerology and OFDM

NR scales subcarrier spacing (SCS) as 15 × 2μ kHz. Common values are 15, 30, 60, 120 and, for specific high-frequency/synchronization cases, 240 kHz. Increasing SCS shortens OFDM symbols and slots, reducing latency but reducing tolerance to delay spread and generally reducing coverage suitability.

Normal cyclic prefix is used for most deployments. The uplink can use CP-OFDM and, where configured, DFT-s-OFDM for coverage/PA-efficiency benefits. Modulation includes QPSK, 16QAM, 64QAM and 256QAM, with additional capability-dependent options in later releases.

7. Frame, slots and symbols

A radio frame is 10 ms. A subframe is 1 ms. The number of slots per subframe scales with numerology. A normal slot contains 14 OFDM symbols; mini-slot transmissions can use fewer symbols and are useful for low-latency scheduling and pre-emption-oriented behavior.

NR's flexible slot structure allows DL/UL direction patterns to be configured for TDD, rather than tying every service to a rigid LTE-style timing model.

8. Resource grid and bandwidth parts

The NR resource grid is organized in resource blocks, subcarriers and OFDM symbols. A physical resource block contains 12 consecutive subcarriers for a given numerology. A resource element is one subcarrier in one OFDM symbol.

Bandwidth Parts (BWPs) let a UE operate on a smaller active bandwidth inside a wider carrier. This reduces UE power/processing requirements and enables flexible adaptation. A UE can have configured DL and UL BWPs, with one active BWP at a time in the normal case.

9. Physical and logical channels

  • PDSCH: downlink shared data.
  • PUSCH: uplink shared data.
  • PDCCH: downlink control/DCI.
  • PUCCH: uplink control such as HARQ-ACK, SR and CSI.
  • PRACH: random-access preambles.
  • PBCH: broadcast channel carrying the MIB.
  • Logical channels include BCCH, PCCH, CCCH, DCCH and DTCH.
  • Transport channels include BCH, DL-SCH, UL-SCH and PCH.

10. Synchronization, SSB, PBCH and system information

The SS/PBCH block (SSB) combines PSS, SSS and PBCH/DM-RS. The UE uses it for synchronization, cell identification, beam selection and acquisition of the MIB. In FR2 and beam-centric deployments, multiple SSBs can represent different beams.

SIB1 provides remaining minimum system information needed for cell access and scheduling of other system information. The UE normally uses the MIB/SIB1 chain before attempting normal access.

11. Random access (RACH)

Random access establishes uplink timing and helps the UE obtain resources to continue access. The classic contention-based procedure is a four-step sequence: Msg1 PRACH preamble → Msg2 RAR → Msg3 scheduled transmission → Msg4 contention resolution. Contention-free access can be configured for cases such as handover.

Beam association matters in NR: PRACH occasions/preambles can be linked with SSBs so that access targets an appropriate beam.

12. NR protocol stack

  • PHY: modulation, coding, MIMO, reference signals, synchronization and radio procedures.
  • MAC: scheduling, HARQ, logical-channel multiplexing and random access control.
  • RLC: segmentation/reassembly and retransmission in AM mode.
  • PDCP: ciphering/integrity, header compression and packet handling.
  • SDAP: maps 5GC QoS flows to data radio bearers.
  • RRC: connection, measurement, mobility and radio configuration control.
  • NAS: UE ↔ core registration, authentication and session management.

13. RRC states and procedures

Key RRC states are RRC_IDLE, RRC_INACTIVE and RRC_CONNECTED. RRC controls configuration, measurements, mobility, security activation, bearer setup and release.

Typical connection establishment is: cell selection → system information → random access → RRC Setup → RRC Setup Complete (with NAS) → security/configuration → connected data transfer. RRC Inactive reduces signalling and resume latency for suitable traffic patterns.

14. MIMO, beamforming, CSI and TCI

Massive MIMO and beamforming are central to NR, especially at higher frequencies. The network uses reference signals such as CSI-RS and SSBs to support channel estimation, beam measurement and CSI feedback.

QCL relationships allow the UE to infer common spatial/delay characteristics between signals. TCI states can associate PDCCH/PDSCH reception with appropriate reference-signal spatial parameters. Beam failure detection and recovery become important as frequency increases.

15. Mobility, measurement and handover

UE measurements can include SS-RSRP, SS-RSRQ and SS-SINR. Measurement events and configured thresholds help the network decide when to move the UE. NR handover can use Xn or core-assisted paths depending on architecture.

Beam-level measurements may influence cell-level mobility. Contention-free random access can make the target-cell access step faster and more reliable when dedicated resources are available.

16. QoS, 5QI and QoS flows

5G replaces the LTE bearer-centric view with a QoS Flow model in the 5GC. A QoS Flow is identified by a QFI. The standardized 5QI conveys QoS characteristics such as priority, packet delay budget and packet error characteristics.

SDAP maps QoS flows to Data Radio Bearers. The network can apply different treatment to conversational voice, video, interactive data, industrial traffic and ordinary best-effort traffic.

17. Registration and PDU Session

Registration establishes the UE's relationship with the 5GC and includes identity, authentication/security and mobility-registration handling. PDU Session Establishment creates user-plane connectivity toward a Data Network through an SMF-selected UPF.

The PDU Session procedure carries parameters such as DNN, S-NSSAI, PDU Session Type, SSC mode and UE 5GSM capabilities. NAS session-management messages are transported through RRC and NGAP toward the core.

18. Network slicing

A network slice is a logical network instance tailored to a service or tenant. The UE requests slice assistance information using S-NSSAI. The AMF uses slice information to select appropriate network functions, while the SMF/UPF provide the session and user-plane realization.

Slice engineering includes isolation, admission, QoS, policy, observability and lifecycle management. A PDU Session is associated with a network slice.

19. Security

5G security separates access-stratum and NAS protection, supports mutual authentication through the 5G-AKA or EAP-based mechanisms, and uses integrity/ciphering algorithms negotiated from UE/network capabilities. Security context establishment is coordinated across NAS and RRC.

Important engineering checks include authentication failures, security-mode failures, unsupported algorithms, identity handling, replay protection, integrity errors and transitions between mobility/security contexts.

20. 5G-Advanced and current evolution

5G did not stop at Release 15. Later releases extend AI/ML support, XR/immersive services, RedCap, sidelink, positioning, NTN, energy saving, mobility enhancements and other radio/core capabilities. Release 19 is a major 5G-Advanced phase while 3GPP work continues to evolve the system.

For a current engineering view, separate normative features from study items and vendor-specific extensions. Do not label a research feature as a finalized 3GPP requirement.

21. NTN, RedCap, sidelink and positioning

  • NTN: extends 5G services through satellite/non-terrestrial platforms and introduces long-delay, Doppler and coverage-specific considerations.
  • RedCap: reduces device complexity and capability for mid-tier IoT/wearable use cases.
  • Sidelink: enables UE-to-UE communication for direct communication and specialized services.
  • Positioning: combines radio measurements and reference signals to estimate location, with accuracy and latency depending on method and deployment.

22. IMS voice, VoNR and fallback

Voice in 5G is normally delivered through IMS using SIP. VoNR keeps the voice session on NR when coverage and QoS support it. During early deployments, EPS fallback can move a voice setup from NR/5GC toward LTE/EPC; other fallback/RAT procedures depend on release and network architecture.

The SIP signalling domain and the NR radio domain should be debugged separately and then correlated: a SIP failure is not automatically an NR failure, and an NR QoS failure may prevent voice even when SIP signalling is correct.

23. Practical troubleshooting workflow

  1. Check serving cell, SSB/beam and radio quality.
  2. Verify MIB/SIB1 and cell-access eligibility.
  3. Check PRACH/RAR and RRC establishment.
  4. Trace NAS Registration and authentication.
  5. Trace PDU Session Establishment and SMF/UPF selection.
  6. Verify QoS Flow/QFI/5QI and DRB mapping.
  7. Check PDCP/RLC/MAC retransmissions, HARQ and scheduling.
  8. For mobility, correlate measurements, RRC handover messages and target-cell RACH.
  9. For voice, correlate SIP/IMS with QoS and fallback signalling.

24. Standards and study references

Primary engineering references include 3GPP TS 23.501 (5GS architecture), TS 23.502 (5GS procedures), TS 38.300 (NR/NG-RAN overall description), TS 38.211/212/213/214 (NR physical layer), TS 38.331 (RRC), TS 38.413 (NGAP), TS 24.501 (5G NAS), and related 3GPP specifications.

This page is a practical learning summary; the normative specification remains the authority for exact requirements and release-specific behavior.
